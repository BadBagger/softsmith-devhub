# The Department of Impossible Complaints — Story Bible

Working reference for a ~2-hour original point-and-click adventure. Tone: clever,
goofy, absurd, adult — closer to a workplace comedy that's gone metaphysical than a
cozy noir. Never cruel, never punching down, never "edgy" as its own reward. Nothing
here imitates or references any existing game, show, composer, actor, or character.

## Tone calibration (v2 — revised after Ch.1 draft read too safe)

Reference point for register only, not content: absurdist, crude-but-clever
point-and-click comedy in the vein of *Deponia*-style adventure games — runaround
bureaucratic logic played dead straight, characters who are actively a bit of a mess,
jokes that come from institutional sadism (forms that require other forms) rather than
whimsy for its own sake. **Nothing is borrowed from that or any other specific
work** — no characters, jokes, plot beats, or dialogue are reused or paraphrased; it's
a tonal north star, not a source.

What "adult" means here: mild profanity used for punch (not filler), drinking/vice as
character texture, messy exes and bad decisions referenced in passing, institutional
dysfunction played for real satirical bite, characters who are flawed and complicated
rather than one-note quirky. It does **not** mean sexual content, gore, or cruelty —
the brief is still "not cruel, not grim, not edgy for adults' sake." The edge comes
from the writing being sharp and the people being real, not from shock value.

Practical rules:
- Every named character gets at least one flaw or complication that isn't played for
  redemption — it's just true about them, like a real adult.
- Bureaucratic obstacles should be actively hostile to the player's patience (a queue
  system for an empty room, a form that requires a form) — the comedy is systemic
  sadism, not cute quirk.
- Profanity budget: used sparingly, for a real beat, never as a tic. "Hell," "damn,"
  "bullshit" are in bounds; nothing harder needed for this tone.
- Every joke should survive being read by someone having a genuinely bad week — if a
  line only works because it's mean to someone, cut it.

## Premise

Reality has paperwork. Somewhere behind the world you can see is a municipal building
where every fact of existence — the weather, the emotional tenor of a room, the value
of a physical constant, the existence of a given Tuesday — is filed, permitted,
licensed, and renewed by career civil servants who mostly do a fine job and occasionally
don't. When something slips through — a permit lapses, a filing goes missing, a day
never gets approved — someone from the Department has to go fix it. Not with magic.
With the correct form, in the correct order, stamped by the correct person.

Mara Finch is that someone.

The inciting incident (Ch.1) looks small: Tuesday, this week, was never approved. By
the end of the game it turns out to be the first visible symptom of one very tired,
very overlooked employee quietly un-filing pieces of the world because nobody noticed
her stop being fine. The game's back half is a mystery in the noir sense (who, why,
what do we do about it) resolved in the game's own idiom: not a fight, a *hearing* —
the correct form, filled out with actual care, is how you fix a person too.

## Cast

### Mara Finch — PROTAGONIST
Investigator, Municipal Bureau of Reality Compliance. Late thirties in spirit if not in
years. Teal trench coat, badge, satchel she never quite unpacks between cases. Tired,
intelligent, dry, humane — the exhaustion of someone who has seen the system fail
people a hundred times and still shows up, not because she's naive about it but because
showing up is the whole job. Her humor is precise, not cruel; she needles the
absurdity of the building, never the people stuck in it. Warmth breaks through the
dryness in small moments — with the pigeon, with anyone visibly having a bad day, and
increasingly with Mr. Quire as the game goes on.

**Complication:** runs on vending-machine coffee that is objectively terrible and
will not hear a word against it. Divorced two years ago from someone named Del who
she brings up constantly while insisting she never thinks about Del. Keeps a mug from
that marriage on her desk out of pure spite ("WORLD'S OKAYEST CASEWORKER" — a gift,
from someone who is no longer speaking to her, still her favorite mug). Good at her
job and privately convinced the job doesn't actually fix anything, which is closer to
true than the Department would like.

Running throughline: Mara used to believe the paperwork *was* the point. Over the
course of the game she relearns that the paperwork is only good insofar as someone
cares enough to get it right — which is also, quietly, the thesis of how she solves
the endgame.

### Mr. Quire — PAPER CLERK, Ground Floor Filing Desk
Built, visibly and literally, from the Department's own paper stock — visor, vest of
form-pockets, a skirt of folded correspondence, a rubber stamp he is never without.
Officious and *sincerely delighted* by procedure; the comedy is that he is never once
being difficult on purpose, he just genuinely believes the form is the love language.
Not an obstacle to be defeated — a colleague to be worked with, who becomes an ally
partway through Act 2 once Mara treats him like one instead of like scenery.

**Complication:** his devotion to procedure isn't quirky, it's load-bearing — he
processes the filings that quietly erase pieces of the world and has decided that
"I only handle the paper" is a load-bearing lie he tells himself so he doesn't have to
be the one who stops it. Play this as genuinely a little unsettling under the cheer in
his early scenes (a beat too long holding the stamp, a joke about "everything means
something today" that he doesn't smile through) before it resolves into real guilt and
real help from Ch.2 on. He is not evil. He is a mid-level employee who found it easier
not to ask questions, which is its own adult, complicated thing to be.

He knows more than he lets on about the building's current wrongness but is
constitutionally unable to editorialize about a colleague's filings — it isn't
procedure. Getting him to finally say something useful is a Chapter 4/5 beat, not a
Chapter 1 one; Ch.1 only cracks the door.

### The Pigeon — Tube Courier, Ground Floor
Terse, self-important, sparse. Delivers inter-office correspondence via the pigeon
tube. Treats the increasingly impossible contents of the building with total
professional indifference. Non-name; "the Pigeon" is its title the way "the Clerk" is
a title, and nobody in-universe finds this strange enough to remark on more than once.

### Petra Vane — the "culprit," Archive of Unfiled Things (introduced Ch.5)
Twenty-two years in the Archive, alone, filing the things nobody wants to look at
directly (a marriage that quietly ended, a childhood house that got sold, the loud
version of a city block before a highway went in). Nobody has filed a form to check on
*her* in about four years. She didn't set out to break Tuesday — she stopped being able
to tell the difference between filing something away and making it stop existing, and
by the time she noticed, it was easier to keep going than to explain. Sympathetic,
funny in a quieter register than the rest of the game, never played as a villain to be
punished — played as a coworker who needs someone to notice her, which is the actual
climax.

### Recurring bit-part civil servants (one scene each, Ch.2–4)
- **Permit Officer Dill** (Weather & Atmosphere) — anxious, over-caffeinated, in charge
  of a sun permit that expired because he was scared to bother his supervisor about the
  renewal.
- **Supervisor Ambrose Coldwell** (Emotional Zoning) — bone-dry bureaucrat who zones
  public moods with total sincerity and no self-awareness about how funny that is.
- **Dr. Yew** (Physical Constants Licensing) — technically brilliant, terrible at
  people, treats gravity's expiring license like a mildly annoying scheduling conflict.

## Structure (target ~2 hours)

| # | Location | Beat | Est. |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Municipal Lobby / Records Annex | Tuesday was never approved | ~20 min |
| 2 | Weather & Atmosphere Permits | Sun's permit lapsed; Quire becomes an ally | ~20 min |
| 3 | Bureau of Emotional Zoning | A citizen stuck in an un-rezoned Grief room | ~20 min |
| 4 | Physical Constants Licensing | Gravity's about to lapse; pattern confirmed as deliberate | ~20 min |
| 5 | Archive of Unfiled Things | Meet Petra; understand why, not just what | ~20 min |
| 6 | Director's Office | The correct form, filled out with care; Tuesday restored | ~20 min |

Each floor follows the same skeleton the prototype already proved out (obtain item →
process it through the floor's specialist → use the result to unlock the next area →
discover a piece of the larger wrongness → a confrontation/resolution beat) so the
puzzle grammar stays consistent even as the content and stakes escalate.

## Style rules for dialogue

- Nobody in the building treats the impossible as impossible — only as *incorrectly
  filed*. That mismatch is the game's comic engine; don't break it for a cheap gag.
- Mara's jokes land on the system, never on a person struggling inside it.
- Mr. Quire is never wrong that procedure matters — he's wrong about it being
  sufficient on its own. The game should never mock him for caring.
- No cruelty toward Petra, ever, including from minor characters. The building's
  culture is exhausted-but-kind, not vicious.
- Reuse "Form 48-B," "Official Grievance of Moderate Importance," and "Tuesday was
  never approved" as recurring callbacks across chapters — running bits earn the
  ending more if they've been seeded early.
